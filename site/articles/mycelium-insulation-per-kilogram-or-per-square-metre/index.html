Per Kilogram or Per Square Metre? Reading a New Swedish Study of Mycelium Insulation

Researchers in Sweden grew insulation from fungi and food-industry residues and report far lower energy and CO2 per kilogram than EPS. Converted to the unit insulation is actually used in, the picture changes.

SustainArc27 November 20269 min read

Norsk sammendrag

Per kilo eller per kvadratmeter? En ny svensk studie av mycelisolasjon

Forskere fra Stockholms universitet, Chalmers, Luleå tekniske universitet og KTH dyrket isolasjonsmateriale av sopp (Ganoderma lucidum) på hvetekli, risstrå og kaffegrut. Materialet var vannavvisende på overflaten, tålte trykk bedre enn EPS og ga lavere varmeavgivelse i konkalorimeter. Studien oppgir opptil 89 prosent lavere energibruk og 72 prosent lavere CO2-utslipp per kilo enn EPS. Men prøvene var 6 til 20 ganger tyngre per kubikkmeter enn vanlig EPS og ledet varme 2,5 til 3,5 ganger så godt. Regnet om til samme isolasjonsevne per kvadratmeter snus sammenligningen. Artikkelen forklarer hvorfor, og hva som må testes før slike materialer kan brukes i norske bygg.

In December 2025 a group of Swedish researchers published a study of insulation grown from fungi on food-industry residues. The headline numbers are striking: up to 89 % lower energy demand and 72 % lower CO2 emissions than expanded polystyrene (EPS) [1]. The study is careful work, and it is open access. But those two percentages are per kilogram of material, and insulation is not bought, designed or regulated per kilogram. This article sets out what the study shows, converts its figures to the unit that matters for a wall, and lists what would need to be tested before a material like this could be used in a Nordic building.

What the researchers did

The team, from Stockholm University, Chalmers University of Technology, Luleå University of Technology and KTH, grew the fungus Ganoderma lucidum on four substrates: fine and medium wheat bran, rice straw and spent coffee grounds. The cultures were grown at 25 °C and 30 °C and then dried at 60 °C, which kills the fungus and leaves a solid composite [1].

They measured the samples' microstructure, density, compression and tension, surface water repellency, thermal stability, thermal conductivity and reaction to fire. Compression was tested to ASTM D1621, reaction to fire with a cone calorimeter to ISO 5660-1 at 35 kW/m², and thermal conductivity with a modified hot-plate method on samples about 5 mm thick [1].

What the study shows

The results, all from laboratory samples, are these [1]:

  • Density ranged from 172 to 330 kg/m³, depending on substrate and growth temperature.
  • Thermal conductivity ranged from 0.099 to 0.124 W/(m·K). The authors note that this is higher than the 0.045 to 0.085 W/(m·K) reported in the literature for other Ganoderma lucidum composites, and higher than the 0.036 to 0.046 W/(m·K) they cite for EPS.
  • Compression: wheat bran composites reached up to 449 kPa at 30 % strain, higher than the EPS reference in the same test. In tension they were stiffer than EPS but more brittle, with strain at break of 5 to 20 % against 84 % for EPS.
  • Surface water repellency: water contact angles of 106° to 120°, which the authors attribute to the mycelium skin covering the surface.
  • Fire: in the cone calorimeter, the wheat bran composites had a peak heat release rate of 112 to 115 kW/m², against 377 kW/m² for the EPS reference. The coffee-ground composite reached 163 kW/m² with the highest total heat release of the composites, and the rice straw composite ignited quickly, after 6.5 seconds.
  • Energy and carbon: using the laboratory cabinet's specified energy use and a global average electricity intensity of 0.346 kg CO2 per kWh, the authors estimate 5.6 to 12.3 MJ and 0.5 to 1.2 kg CO2 eq. per kg of composite, against about 82 MJ and 3 kg CO2 eq. per kg for EPS taken from the literature.

The authors are clear that the sustainability figures are indicative. They write that the study "does not primarily aim to assess the sustainability and circularity of the process", and that energy use at industrial scale could be lower [1].

Why per kilogram is the wrong unit

An insulation layer has one job: to provide a given thermal resistance, R, over an area of wall or roof. That is why the European product category rules for insulation EPDs, EN 16783, define the declared unit for boards as 1 m² of product at a stated thermal resistance [3]. A Norwegian EPD for EPS boards shows how this works in practice: its declared unit is 1 m² with R = 1 m²K/W [2].

For a layer with R = 1 m²K/W, the thickness needed is simply the thermal conductivity in metres, and the mass per square metre is that thickness times the density. Using the study's own measurements for two of its composites, and the EPD's declared values for the lightest EPS board, the comparison looks like this. The calculation is ours; the inputs are from the sources given.

Material (R = 1 m²K/W) Conductivity, W/(m·K) Density, kg/m³ Thickness Mass per m²
Mycelium on medium wheat bran, 25 °C [1] 0.099 245 99 mm 24.3 kg
Mycelium on rice straw, 25 °C [1] 0.109 172 109 mm 18.7 kg
EPS board, 16 kg/m³ [2] 0.040 16 40 mm 0.64 kg

The mycelium layer needs about two and a half times the thickness and roughly 30 to 40 times the mass to insulate as well as the EPS board.

Now apply the study's indicative carbon figure of 0.5 to 1.2 kg CO2 eq. per kg. For the lightest composite, 18.7 kg/m² gives about 9 to 22 kg CO2 eq. per square metre at R = 1. The EPS EPD reports 1.58 kg CO2 eq. for production (modules A1 to A3) per square metre at R = 1 [2]. On these numbers the mycelium samples come out higher, not lower.

Two cautions keep this from being the final word. First, the figures are not like for like: the mycelium estimate covers only the energy use of the laboratory cabinet used to produce the samples, while the EPD covers raw materials and factory production under EN 15804. Second, a full comparison would include end of life and stored biogenic carbon. The same EPD reports a further 2.32 kg CO2 eq. per square metre for EPS waste processing (module C3) [2], and the mycelium composite contains carbon taken up by plants, which the study does not quantify. A study from Aalto University that modelled mycelium-wood insulation in renovation of the Helsinki housing stock found a large carbon storage potential, while noting that the result depends heavily on factors beyond the insulation itself [5].

What the conversion does show is that the per-kilogram percentages cannot be used to claim a climate benefit for a wall. The decisive variables are density and thermal conductivity. A composite with the same carbon per kilogram but a density closer to 100 kg/m³ and a conductivity near 0.05 W/(m·K) would need about 5 kg per square metre at R = 1, and the comparison would look very different. The study's authors point in the same direction: they write that density reduction "remains crucial" [1].

Fire: a promising result, not a classification

The cone calorimeter results are useful for comparing formulations, and the lower peak heat release than EPS is a real finding. But a cone test is not a reaction-to-fire class, and it is the class that decides where a product may be used.

In Norway, the guidance to § 11-9 of TEK17 [6] treats any insulation that does not meet at least class A2-s1,d0 as combustible. Under the pre-accepted performances, insulation must meet A2-s1,d0 unless one of the listed exceptions applies, and combustible insulation can only be used where the building part still performs its fire function and the insulation is protected so that it does not contribute to fire spread, for example by being fully covered or cast in [4]. A composite of wheat bran and fungal biomass is organic, and the study does not claim any class. Until a product has been tested and classified, it should be treated as combustible insulation under these rules.

What the study does not answer

For a Nordic building envelope, the open questions are mostly about moisture and durability:

  • Moisture uptake and mould. The study measured surface contact angle, which describes how water beads on the skin. It did not measure water vapour sorption, capillary uptake, behaviour at high relative humidity over time, or resistance to mould growth. These decide whether a bio-based layer stays dry and stable inside a wall.
  • Declared thermal performance. The conductivity values come from a modified hot-plate method on thin samples [1]. Products on the market declare their conductivity under their product standard, as the EPS EPD does with its reference to EN 13163 [2]. A declared value at a defined moisture content would be needed for any building calculation.
  • Scale and variability. Several properties varied widely between batches; the tensile strength of the coffee-ground composite, for example, was reported as 317 ± 127 kPa [1]. Industrial production would need to show consistent density and conductivity from batch to batch.
  • Brittleness. The authors note brittle failure and suggest work on ductility [1], which matters for handling, cutting and fixing on site.

What the study contributes

The Swedish study is valuable because it links substrate, fungal growth and properties in one dataset, and because it shows that wheat bran gives a denser, more continuous mycelial network than straw or coffee grounds, and more balanced fire behaviour than the coffee-ground composite [1]. It is also a good example of why claims about bio-based materials need to be read in the right unit.

What to check before specifying a bio-based insulation

The following is our recommendation, not a regulatory requirement. It applies to mycelium composites and to any new bio-based insulation product.

  1. Ask for figures in the declared unit. Environmental data should be per square metre at a stated thermal resistance, ideally in an EPD to EN 15804 and EN 16783 [3]. Per-kilogram figures cannot be compared between insulation products.
  2. Ask for declared values, not research values. Thermal conductivity and density should be the values declared for the product on the market, at a defined moisture content.
  3. Check the reaction-to-fire class against the position in the building. Compare the product's class with TEK17 § 11-9 and its guidance for the building's fire class and the intended use [4]. Without a class, treat the product as combustible insulation.
  4. Ask for moisture and mould documentation for the actual build-up. Water vapour sorption, behaviour at high relative humidity and mould resistance matter more in a Nordic wall than surface water repellency.
  5. Read laboratory results as direction, not product data. A promising study shows what could be developed. It does not show what can be built with today.
