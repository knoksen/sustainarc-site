Dry Enough to Close? Measuring Construction Moisture in Timber Buildings Under TEK17

The moisture rule in TEK17 is one sentence long. Whether a timber building meets it is decided on site, at the moment someone closes a layer, and it can only be shown with measurements.

Norsk sammendrag

Tørt nok til å lukke? Måling av byggfukt i trebygg etter TEK17

TEK17 § 13-14 krever at produkter og konstruksjoner er så tørre ved innbygging eller forsegling at det ikke oppstår soppdannelse, nedbrytning eller økt avgassing. Veiledningen angir under 20 vektprosent fukt i trevirke, og under 15 vektprosent der uttørkingsevnen er lav, og sier at fuktinnholdet må måles. Regelen sier lite om hvor fuktig treet kan bli underveis. Erfaringer fra norske massivtreprosjekter viser at bygging uten telt kan gå bra med tett oppfølging, men at det krever arbeid. Artikkelen foreslår en enkel protokoll: fuktplan, klassifisering av bygningsdeler, målepunkter, stoppunkter før lukking, logg og avvikshåndtering.

Every timber building is wet at some point during construction. Rain falls on an open slab, snow lies on a roof element over a weekend, and a wall is closed on a Friday afternoon because the insulation crew is booked for Monday. The regulation does not forbid any of this. What it requires is that the timber is dry enough at the moment it is built in or sealed.

That moment is easy to miss. This article sets out what TEK17 says about construction moisture, what it leaves open, what recent Norwegian projects with cross-laminated timber (CLT) have shown, and a simple protocol for measuring and recording moisture before anything is closed.

What the regulation says

TEK17 § 13-14, Byggfukt, is a functional requirement. Products and structures must be so dry when they are built in or sealed that problems with mould growth, decay of organic materials or increased emissions do not occur [1][2].

The guidance from the Norwegian Building Authority (DiBK) adds the numbers most people know. Under the pre-accepted performances, timber must contain less than 20 % moisture by weight to avoid fungal attack. In structures with low drying capacity, for example structures against the ground, the moisture content of the timber must be below 15 % by weight before it is built in. The guidance also notes that drying can take a long time, especially one-sided drying of concrete, and states that the moisture content must be measured to document that the requirement is met [1].

Two points are worth stating precisely. The legal requirement is the functional sentence in § 13-14, not the 20 % figure. And the guidance gives no numerical limit for concrete; it refers to a critical value for the material combination in question, for example where PVC flooring is glued to a screed [1].

Why the documentation question is current

The 20 % and 15 % figures are pre-accepted performances. A project that follows them is assumed to meet the requirement. A project that chooses another route must show by analysis that the functional requirement is met.

On 3 June 2026 DiBK published a proposal to change the guidance to chapter 2 of TEK17, which deals with how compliance is documented. According to DiBK, the change is meant to make clearer that analysis is a route already open in the regulation, and that analysis is to be assessed against the functional requirement rather than against the pre-accepted performances. DiBK states that the proposal does not change the level of the technical requirements, and that guidance is not a requirement. The deadline for comments was 16 June 2026, and DiBK said it would consider further changes to chapter 2 until 15 December 2026 [3]. At the time of writing, in November 2026, we have not confirmed the final wording, so check the current guidance to § 2-2 on dibk.no.

For construction moisture, our reading is practical. Most projects will keep the 20 % and 15 % limits, because they are simple to test and widely understood. But if a project wants to close a structure at a higher moisture content, for example because a specific build-up can dry in a known direction, the argument has to be a documented analysis of mould and decay risk for that build-up, not an informal judgement on site.

What the rule leaves open

The regulation sets a condition at the moment of closing. It does not say how wet timber may become during construction, or for how long. A 2019 master's thesis at NMBU, based on measurements on a CLT building erected without weather protection, made exactly this point: the requirement of less than 20 % applies when the part is closed, but the permitted moisture level and duration of exposure before closing is not specified [6].

The thesis found the highest readings above floor slabs and below cut-outs in vertical elements, and concluded that precipitation and variations in relative humidity matter, so the local climate should be quantified before the project starts [6]. Those are the places a measurement plan should look first.

What Norwegian CLT projects show

The best documented recent case is the ZEB Laboratory at NTNU in Trondheim, a four-storey building in CLT and glulam built without a weather-protection tent. SINTEF and NTNU researchers describe a strategy of local protective measures, removal of free water after rain and regular measurements at 50 points. No wooden surfaces were encapsulated until a moisture content below 15 % by weight had been measured. Moisture levels fell quickly once the envelope was closed, and the authors call the strategy generally successful. In the first year they observed gaps between flooring and skirting boards, which suggests shrinkage as the timber dried [4].

In a later commentary the same researchers report no significant moisture damage, based on wood moisture, air quality, airtightness and visual inspection. They also write that the approach was labour-intensive in monitoring, assessment and validation, and that more knowledge is needed to decide what level of moisture monitoring is sufficient [5]. That is an honest summary: building without a tent can work, but it moves the effort from the tent to the measurement log.

Earlier work by Mycoteam presented at SINTEF's national moisture seminar in 2018 gives two useful warnings. In a roof project, a CLT deck dried to 20 % in three and a half weeks after the membrane was removed. In a laboratory test of compact roofs, CLT without a vapour barrier stayed wet for a long time, and drying only started after four months. The presentation's recommendations included that larger projects should have a person responsible for moisture, that insulation should not be installed before the roof is tight, and that moisture monitoring should be set up when in doubt [7].

A measure-before-close protocol

The following is our recommendation, not a regulatory requirement. It turns the one-sentence rule into hold points that can be checked.

1. Name a person and write a moisture plan

Appoint one person responsible for construction moisture, with authority to stop a closing operation. The plan should state the weather protection strategy, the drying route for each build-up and the acceptance limits that apply.

2. Classify parts by drying capacity

Sort the building parts into two groups. Parts that can dry after closing use the 20 % limit. Parts with low drying capacity, such as structures against the ground, compact roofs and parts sealed on both sides, use 15 % [1]. If the project wants a different limit for a specific build-up, write down the analysis behind it.

3. Place measurement points where water collects

Use the known wet spots: slab edges and the top of slabs, the underside of openings and cut-outs, element joints, end grain, and the lowest point of each element [6]. Mark the points on drawings so the same spots are measured each time.

4. Use a stated method

Handheld resistance meters estimate the moisture content of timber; the European standard EN 13183-2 describes this non-destructive method, while EN 13183-1 describes the oven-dry method, which is the reference [8][9]. Record the instrument, the species and temperature correction used, and the measuring depth. When a reading is borderline or disputed, take a sample for oven-dry testing.

5. Make closing a hold point

No layer that limits drying is installed until the readings for that area are recorded and below the limit. This is the rule the ZEB Laboratory used, and it is simple to audit [4][5].

6. Keep a log that someone else can read

For every measurement: date, location, depth, reading, temperature, instrument, person and a photo. For every deviation: what was done, such as extra drying, opening a layer or changing the sequence, and the new reading.

7. Hand the record over

Include the moisture log, the deviations and the final readings in the documentation for operation and maintenance. If a problem appears years later, the log is the only evidence of what the timber looked like when it was closed.
