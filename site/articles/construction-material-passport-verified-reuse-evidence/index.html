The Material Passport Is Not a Reuse Permit: Building a Verified Second-Life Record

A QR code can tell us where a component came from. It cannot, by itself, tell us whether that component is safe to install again.

SustainArc15 November 202610 min read

Norsk sammendrag

Materialpasset er ikke en ombrukstillatelse: slik dokumenteres et bygningsdels andre liv

EUs nye byggevareforordning (EU) 2024/3110 legger grunnlaget for digitale produktpass for byggevarer, men gjennomføres trinnvis, og forordningen er ennå ikke tatt inn i EØS-avtalen eller norsk rett. Et produktpass beskriver en produkttype; det sier ikke om en demontert bygningsdel fortsatt holder for en ny bruk. Artikkelen foreslår en sporbar «andre-liv-journal» i seks porter: identifiser og sett i karantene, hent originaldokumentasjon, inspiser og klassifiser usikkerhet, test for den nye bruken, godkjenn en bestemt bruk, og bevar revisjonssporet. Til slutt følger et 30-dagers pilotopplegg for et reelt prosjekt.

Circular construction needs better evidence, not just better labels.

Why the evidence matters

Construction and demolition waste accounts for more than a third of all waste generated in the EU, according to the European Commission. Recovery varies greatly between countries, from less than 10 % to over 90 %, and definitions differ. The Commission also notes that waste not separated at source can contain hazardous materials such as asbestos, which pose particular risks and impede recycling [1].

The circular ambition is therefore not simply to move more tonnes out of landfill. It is to keep useful products and components in applications where their condition, performance and legal status can be established.

A steel beam, facade panel or raised-floor unit may look suitable for a second life. The next project still needs answers. What exactly is it? What loads or exposure has it seen? Has it been altered? Which tests and approvals are relevant? Who accepts responsibility for its new use? A digital record can connect these questions. It cannot answer them automatically.

What the new EU construction-products rules change

Regulation (EU) 2024/3110 modernises the European framework for marketing construction products. The Council adopted it on 5 November 2024 and highlighted digital product passports, environmental information and circularity as key elements [2]. The legal text establishes a framework for a construction digital product passport system, covering identification, access, integrity, security and relevant product information [3].

Implementation is staged. The Commission must specify the passport system through further acts, so it would be wrong to assume that every product on a site already carries a legally required, operational EU passport. Belgium's federal economy ministry, for example, explains that detailed arrangements are still being developed and that manufacturer obligations follow the relevant delegated act and transition period [4].

In Norway, the new regulation is not yet incorporated into the EEA Agreement or Norwegian law, according to the government's EEA notes from 2026 [5]. Norwegian rules already push in the same direction: since 1 July 2022, materials must be mapped for reuse before larger works on existing buildings, and the product documentation rules were adjusted to make it easier to place used building products on the market, while the documentation requirement in TEK17 applies to new and used products alike [6].

A manufacturer's product passport and a building owner's material inventory are also not the same record. A product passport describes an identified product type and its declarations. A second-life record must also capture installation history, actual condition, dismantling, testing, storage and acceptance for a specific new use.

A passport is an index to evidence, not a substitute for it

A useful record separates four kinds of information:

  • Identity: manufacturer, product family, model, serial or batch number where available, dimensions, and a durable identifier.
  • Declared performance: the declaration of performance, specifications, intended use and limitations, tied to the right regulatory and standards context.
  • Life history: building and location, exposure, repairs, modifications, inspections and removal method. Historical information is often incomplete. Uncertainty should stay visible rather than being filled by assumption.
  • Second-life suitability: condition survey, sampling and testing, assessment against the proposed new duty, legal classification and approvals, and the professional decision that authorises reuse.

The last category is always project-specific. A valid historical declaration does not prove that a recovered component still performs the same after decades of service or after dismantling.

The second-life evidence chain

We propose six gates for a small, auditable reuse pilot. They are recommended process controls, not stages required by Regulation (EU) 2024/3110.

Gate 1 — Identify and quarantine. Give each component a salvage ID, photograph it, record its location and keep it apart from unassessed stock. Screen for obvious hazards and agree a safe handling plan before any intrusive work.

Gate 2 — Recover the original evidence. Collect manufacturer records, declarations, drawings, maintenance logs, installation dates and any digital product passport. Record which sources are primary documents and which are unverified recollections.

Gate 3 — Inspect and classify uncertainty. Document corrosion, cracking, deformation, moisture, coatings, connections, contamination and missing provenance as relevant to the material. Mark each claim as verified, inferred, unknown or not applicable.

Gate 4 — Test for the proposed duty. Decide, with competent professionals, whether testing, calculations, certification or other conformity evidence is needed. This depends on the material, the jurisdiction and the intended use. A component suitable for a non-structural use may not be acceptable for a load-bearing one.

Gate 5 — Approve a defined destination. Link the assessment to the new project's design conditions, the applicable building regulations and the responsible decision-maker. An approval for one use should not silently transfer to another.

Gate 6 — Preserve the audit trail. Store the identifier, evidence versions, test results, decisions, restrictions, installed location and future inspection needs in a controlled information environment. Future custodians must be able to tell original declarations from later assessments.

Why tonnage statistics can mislead

Ireland's Environmental Protection Agency reported about 7.9 million tonnes of construction and demolition waste in 2024, of which 77 % was soil and stones. Of the non-hazardous waste excluding soil and stones, 81 % was recycled or backfilled [7].

Figures like these are useful for material flows, but they do not show how many components were directly reused at equal or higher value. A project that claims circular success should distinguish direct reuse, preparation for reuse, recycling, other recovery, backfilling and disposal.

The same applies to carbon claims. Reusing a component may avoid some manufacturing impacts, but transport, cleaning, testing, refurbishment, service life and replacement scenarios all change the result. A comparison needs a declared functional basis and transparent boundaries. A passport alone does not establish a carbon saving.

An illustrative record

Consider a hypothetical recovered facade bracket:

Evidence field Illustrative entry
Salvage ID SA-PILOT-BR-001
Original product identity Manufacturer unknown; profile and dimensions measured
Source Building A, facade grid C4
Condition Visual inspection complete; coating composition unknown
Evidence gap No original declaration or batch trace
Proposed new use Non-structural demonstration assembly
Required decision Material identification and suitability review by a competent professional
Release status Hold — not approved for installation

This is an invented teaching example, not a tested component. Its value lies in showing why a visible hold state is more trustworthy than a polished but incomplete digital profile.

A 30-day pilot for a real project

Week 1 — Choose a narrow material stream. Select a limited batch from a controlled source, name the owners and safety responsibilities, and define what a complete evidence record looks like.

Week 2 — Build the traceability baseline. Assign IDs, collect records, map provenance and document what is missing. A barcode or QR code does not validate the evidence behind it.

Week 3 — Test the assurance process. Ask competent reviewers to assess selected components for one intended use. Log every rejection, every request for more evidence and every change of permitted use.

Week 4 — Review cost and outcome. Measure documentation completeness, components assessed, acceptance and rejection rates, labour hours, testing costs and confirmed destinations. Estimate environmental effects only with stated comparative assumptions.

A successful pilot may conclude that some components should not be reused. That is still a useful result if it prevents an unsafe decision and shows what evidence is missing.

Design for privacy, interoperability and continuity

The EU framework stresses interoperable formats, controlled access, integrity and protection of commercially sensitive information [3]. A project record should follow the same principles. It should not expose occupants' personal data or security-sensitive building details just because a QR code is convenient.

Prefer portable identifiers and structured exports over a proprietary database that disappears when a contract ends. Keep the original source documents, record versions, and separate factual observations from assessments and approvals.

The next step is evidence-aware circularity

A digital passport is a promising way to make product information accessible. The harder task is to keep the evidence needed for decisions after a product has been installed, used, removed and offered for a new purpose.

For architects and engineers, that means linking circular design to inspection, performance, regulation and professional accountability. For contractors, it means planning selective dismantling and traceability before materials become anonymous waste. For owners, it means treating reliable records as part of long-term asset value.

The principle is simple: identify the product, preserve its history, verify its condition, assess its new duty, and record who authorised the decision. A passport can help connect the chain. It cannot replace it.
