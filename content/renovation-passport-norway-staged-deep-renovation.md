Very few owners renovate a whole building at once. The roof is done when it leaks, the windows when they rot, the heating system when it fails. Each step can be sensible on its own and still make the next one harder or more expensive. A new window set in the old wall plane may end up in the wrong position when the facade is insulated five years later. A tighter envelope without better ventilation can raise indoor humidity.

A renovation passport is a way to plan the whole sequence before the first step. The EU has made it part of its buildings directive. Norway has not, at least not yet. The idea is useful regardless.

## What the EU has decided

The recast Energy Performance of Buildings Directive, Directive (EU) 2024/1275, defines a renovation passport as a tailored roadmap for the deep renovation of a specific building [1]. Article 12 requires Member States to introduce a renovation passport scheme by 29 May 2026, based on a common framework set out in Annex VIII [1].

The main features are these [1]:

- It is voluntary for owners, unless a Member State decides to make it mandatory.
- It is issued by a qualified or certified expert after an on-site visit, in a digital format that can also be printed.
- The owner should be offered a discussion with the expert about the best steps towards a zero-emission building well before 2050.
- It may be issued together with the energy performance certificate. In that case it replaces the certificate's recommendations.
- Member States must keep it affordable and consider financial support for vulnerable households.

Annex VIII lists what every passport must contain: the current energy performance; a graphical roadmap of the steps in a staged deep renovation; relevant national rules and their application dates; a short explanation of the best sequence of steps; and for each step, the measures, the estimated energy savings, the estimated reduction in operational greenhouse gas emissions, the estimated energy bill savings with the cost assumptions stated, and the expected energy class afterwards. It must also cover district heating options, expected renewable self-consumption, general information on circularity and whole-life emissions, wider benefits such as comfort and indoor climate, available funding and where to get advice [1].

Optional content includes indicative timing, estimated costs, payback periods with and without support, expected lifetimes and maintenance costs, and a module on how adaptable the spaces are to future needs [1].

## Where Norway stands

The 2024 directive is not part of the EEA Agreement. The Ministry of Energy held a public hearing on it with a deadline of 5 January 2026, as input to its assessment in the EEA process. The previous amending directive from 2018 was only incorporated into the EEA Agreement on 11 July 2025 [2]. Whether, when and with which adaptations the 2024 directive will apply in Norway is still open [2][3]. There is therefore no Norwegian renovation passport scheme today.

Several related things have changed, though:

- **Energy labelling.** A new energy labelling scheme took effect on 1 January 2026, with NS 3031:2025 as the calculation basis [4]. Labels issued before and after that date are not directly comparable [8], which matters when a passport compares a building's state over time.
- **Energy advice.** Enova supports energy advice for homeowners with 25 % of approved costs, up to NOK 5,000. The advice must produce an energy plan with cost-effective measures for the envelope or the technical systems, and owners can ask for a plan that sets priorities and an order of work for a staged upgrade. Applications must be approved before work starts [5].
- **Proposed requirements for existing buildings.** In February 2026 the Norwegian Building Authority proposed, among other changes, separate energy requirements for existing buildings. That hearing also closed on 5 May 2026 [6].
- **Reuse.** Since 1 July 2022, materials must be mapped for reuse before larger works on existing buildings [7].

In other words, the pieces of a renovation passport exist in Norway, but nobody is required to put them together.

## Why the order of steps matters

The sequence is where a passport earns its keep. A few rules of thumb from building physics:

- **Envelope before plant.** Size a heat pump or district heating connection after deciding the envelope upgrades. Otherwise the system is sized for losses that will later disappear.
- **Insulation decides the window position.** If external insulation is planned, place new windows so they line up with the future insulation layer and can be connected airtightly to it. A window replaced first in the old plane often becomes a thermal bridge and a moisture detail later.
- **Airtightness and ventilation go together.** Tightening the envelope reduces uncontrolled air change. Plan the ventilation upgrade in the same step or the step before, to keep indoor humidity and air quality under control.
- **Check drying before adding insulation inside.** Internal insulation and new vapour barriers change how an old wall dries. Assess moisture and construction before choosing the build-up. Bio-based materials can work well, but the wall must keep them dry, as we discussed in [Bio-Based Insulation in Nordic Buildings](https://jarlhalla.com/bio-based-insulation-nordic-moisture-performance/).
- **Survey before you open up.** Map hazardous materials and reuse potential before demolition work, not during it.

None of these are new. The value of a passport is that they are decided once, for the whole building, instead of being rediscovered by each contractor.

## A renovation passport light

Until a national scheme exists, a simpler document can do most of the work. We use eight parts, built on Annex VIII and adapted to Norwegian practice:

1. **Baseline.** Energy certificate, condition survey of the envelope and technical systems, moisture and rot findings, hazardous materials, photos of concealed layers where they have been opened.
2. **Goals.** What the owner wants: lower bills, comfort, more space, a sale, a target energy class, lower emissions. Goals decide priorities.
3. **Sequence.** The steps in order, with the reason for the order and the interfaces between steps.
4. **Step cards.** For each step: measures, material options, estimated energy savings and energy class afterwards, a cost range, and what must be verified on site. Estimates are labelled as estimates, with assumptions stated.
5. **Rules check.** Whether a step needs a building application, which TEK17 requirements apply to the work, and any restrictions for protected buildings.
6. **Materials and circularity.** Reuse from the building itself, low-emission alternatives, and products to avoid for moisture or health reasons.
7. **Funding.** Current Enova and municipal schemes, checked against their terms on the day the step is planned.
8. **Record.** Where documentation is kept, so that the next owner or contractor can see what is behind the cladding.

The document should be short enough to use. A few pages and a timeline are better than a report that is never opened again.

## What a passport cannot do

A plan made today rests on today's prices, rules and support schemes. All three will change over a ten-year renovation. The estimates in a passport are indications, not guarantees, and the plan should be reviewed before each step. Nor does a passport replace the energy certificate, a building application or the responsible applicant's own assessment.
